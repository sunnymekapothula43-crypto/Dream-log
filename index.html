Why Sleep Matters

Sleep Is Your Body's Nightly Reset

Quality sleep is not wasted time. While you rest, your brain sorts memories, your body repairs tissue and your hormones rebalance — setting you up for the day ahead.

Better Mental Health

Restful sleep helps regulate mood and stress hormones, lowering irritability and anxiety and making it easier to cope with daily challenges.

Improved Memory

During deep and REM sleep, the brain consolidates what you learned during the day, which improves recall, focus and problem-solving.

Physical Recovery

Growth hormone peaks in deep sleep, repairing muscles and tissues, while a well-rested immune system fights off illness more effectively.

Better Daily Energy

Waking up refreshed means steadier energy, sharper reactions and fewer afternoon slumps — without relying on extra cups of coffee.

Sleep Hygiene Tips

Six Habits for Deeper, Calmer Sleep

Small, consistent changes to your daily habits and environment make the biggest difference to how well you sleep.

01

Maintain a Consistent Sleep Schedule

Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, including weekends. A steady rhythm trains your internal body clock so falling asleep becomes easier.

02

Avoid Screens Before Bedtime

Blue light from phones and laptops delays the release of melatonin. Switch off screens 30–60 minutes before bed and choose a calm offline activity instead.

03

Keep Your Bedroom Comfortable

Aim for a cool, dark and quiet room — around 18–20 °C is ideal for most people. Blackout curtains, earplugs and a supportive mattress all help.

04

Avoid Caffeine Late in the Day

Caffeine can stay in your system for six hours or more. Enjoy coffee, tea and energy drinks before early afternoon so they don't keep you awake at night.

05

Exercise Regularly

Thirty minutes of daily movement helps you fall asleep faster and sleep more deeply. Try to finish intense workouts at least two hours before bedtime.

06

Avoid Heavy Meals Before Bed

Large, rich or spicy meals late at night can cause discomfort and heartburn. Have dinner two to three hours before bed and keep evening snacks light.

Bedtime Routine

A Simple Wind-Down Routine

Follow these steps each evening to signal to your body that it's time to rest. Adjust the times to match your own schedule.

  1. 9:00 PM

    Finish Heavy Meals

    Wrap up dinner and larger snacks so your body has time to digest before you lie down.

  2. 9:15 PM

    Reduce Screen Time

    Put your phone on charge outside the bedroom, dim the lights and switch to night mode on any devices.

  3. 9:30 PM

    Take a Warm Shower

    A warm shower raises your skin temperature; the gentle cool-down afterwards helps trigger sleepiness.

  4. 9:45 PM

    Read a Book

    Read a few pages of a printed book or journal your thoughts to quiet a busy mind.

  5. 10:00 PM

    Prepare for Sleep

    Turn off the lights, take a few slow, deep breaths and let your body relax into sleep.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the most common questions about sleep hygiene.

What is sleep hygiene?

Sleep hygiene is the set of daily habits and bedroom conditions that support consistent, good-quality sleep. It includes your schedule, evening routine, diet, activity level and sleep environment.

How many hours of sleep do I need?

Most adults need 7–9 hours of sleep per night. Teenagers generally need 8–10 hours. The right amount is the one that lets you wake up feeling refreshed without an alarm most days.

Is it okay to catch up on sleep during the weekend?

Sleeping in occasionally can ease short-term tiredness, but it doesn't fully repay a week of lost sleep. Large weekend shifts can also confuse your body clock, so keep your wake-up time within about an hour of your weekday schedule.

What should I do if I can't fall asleep?

If you're still awake after about 20 minutes, get out of bed and do something quiet and relaxing in dim light, such as reading, until you feel sleepy. Avoid checking the time or your phone.

Are short naps good or bad for sleep?

A short nap of 20–30 minutes in the early afternoon can boost alertness. Longer or late-afternoon naps can make it harder to fall asleep at night.

When should I see a doctor about my sleep?

Talk to a healthcare professional if poor sleep lasts for several weeks, if you snore loudly or gasp during sleep, or if you feel very sleepy during the day despite getting enough hours in bed.
